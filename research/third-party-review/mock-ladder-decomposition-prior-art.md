# Prior-Art Review: Breadth-First Mock Skeleton, Frozen Interfaces, Glue Leaves, Top-Down Decomposition

> Research subagent report, 2026-07-13, commissioned for the second mid-flight third-party review.
> External research only. Sister reports in this dir (agent-orchestration, cooperative-codegen, git-backed-store, rationale-capture, spec-first-discipline).

## 1. Walking skeleton / tracer bullets: the record is for one path, not the whole tree

Cockburn's original formulation (Writing Effective Use Cases, 2000; popularized via 97 Things Every Software Architect Should Know) is explicitly narrow: "a tiny implementation of the system that performs a small end-to-end function," linking the main architectural components so "the architecture and the functionality can then evolve in parallel." Freeman & Pryce (GOOS, 2009) sharpen it further: "the thinnest possible slice of real functionality that we can automatically build, deploy, and test end-to-end" — and they stress the skeleton need not use the final architecture. Hunt & Thomas's tracer bullets (Pragmatic Programmer, 1999) are the same idea with an aiming metaphor: one lean-but-complete path of production-quality code through all layers, kept because feedback from firing it refines the aim. All three formulations assume the skeleton is built to learn which parts of the architecture are wrong.

The proposed design — every node's boundary declared and mocked breadth-first before any implementation — is not a walking skeleton in this lineage. Its actual precedent is 1970s top-down structured programming with stubs (Mills, IBM FSD; Wirth's stepwise refinement), where the whole call tree exists as stubs that are incrementally replaced. That record is instructive and mostly negative on the stub side: standard integration-testing literature documents that stub counts grow fast, stubs are hard to make represent realistic conditions, oversimplified stubs hide defects until late ("false positives or false negatives"), and stubs get progressively more complex as siblings become real. Larman & Basili's history of iterative development shows the field moved from whole-tree stubbing to incremental verified builds precisely because intermediate all-stub states verified little. The distinctive value the design keeps — an executable end-to-end artifact from day one — is validated; the record just says the confidence a *fully mocked* skeleton buys is thin, because nothing real has been exercised. A skeleton of mocks "walks" trivially; it cannot fail for architectural reasons, which is the failure the technique exists to surface (deployment, data-flow, latency, impedance mismatches between real components).

## 2. Mock drift and mock hell: well documented, and this design maximizes exposure to it

The failure modes are unusually well attested:

- **Coupling to interaction structure.** The classicist critique of London-school TDD (Fowler's "Is TDD Dead?" conversations with DHH and Kent Beck, 2014; the Detroit-vs-London literature) is that mock-based tests encode *how* collaborators are called, so refactorings that preserve behavior break tests, and passing tests stop meaning working software.
- **Mocks lie / stub rot.** Software Engineering at Google (ch. 13–14) states it plainly: "Mocks become stale. If this mock-based unit test is not visible to the author of the real implementation and the real implementation changes, there is no signal that the test should be updated." Spadini et al.'s MSR 2017 empirical study ("To Mock or Not To Mock?", best-paper track, EMSE extension) found mocks persist for the lifetime of the test suite and production changes routinely force test churn. Rainsberger's "Integrated Tests Are a Scam" argues the complementary point: suites that integrate against doubles cover 2–5% of the interaction space while duplicating each other.
- **GOOS's own guardrail.** The people who wrote the book on mock-driven design added "don't mock what you don't own": hardcoded expectations about an interface you don't control drift and produce false greens. In the proposed tree, a child implementing against sibling mocks is structurally in the "don't own" position for every sibling boundary — the drift mechanism is identical even though ownership is nominally in-repo.
- **Why Pact worked where naive mocking failed.** Consumer-driven contract testing succeeds because of one mechanism: *the same recorded interactions are executed twice* — once as a mock for the consumer, once as a verification suite against the real provider — with a broker making staleness a CI failure rather than a silent divergence. The mock is not an artifact someone wrote; it is a projection of a contract that the real component must continuously satisfy. (Notably, Pact's creators cite Rainsberger's talk as the spark.) Google's in-house alternative, record/replay of real traffic, has the same property: fidelity is inherited from a real implementation, not authored.

Implication for this design: PLAN-authored static mocks have neither property. Nobody consumes them to generate a verification obligation, and nothing replays them against the real leaf when it lands. The 2026 arXiv study "Are Coding Agents Generating Over-Mocked Tests?" (arXiv 2602.00409) adds an LLM-specific twist: coding agents already over-mock, producing suites that pass against fictions. The single highest-value amendment available from the record is: **every sibling-facing mock must double as a contract suite that runs red/green against the real implementation the moment it exists**, with drift a scheduler-visible fault.

## 3. Interface freeze: the record says interfaces do not survive implementation contact

- **Parnas 1972** ("On the Criteria...") gives the decomposition criterion — hide design decisions likely to change — but **Parnas & Clements 1986** ("A Rational Design Process: How and Why to Fake It", IEEE TSE) is the direct verdict on this design's premise: a rational top-down process is impossible because "many other facts needed for design only become known during implementation, and some things learned can invalidate the design and require backtracking." Their prescription is to *fake* the rational structure in documentation while accepting that the actual process backtracks. A scheduler that treats boundary change as an exceptional "restructuring event" is building the fake into the mechanism and will pay for it as friction.
- **IDL-first at scale.** Henning's "The Rise and Fall of CORBA" (ACM Queue, 2006) attributes failure substantially to interfaces standardized without implementation feedback — no reference implementation required, yielding "castle-in-the-air specifications," kitchen-sink interfaces, and 200 lines of IDL where 30 sufficed. Interfaces authored before implementation knowledge trend fat and wrong.
- **Empirically, even shipped interfaces churn.** Raemaekers et al. (Maven Central, ~22k libraries / 100k+ jars): roughly one third of releases introduce at least one breaking change; ~15% violate semver by breaking in minor/patch releases (22.8% in Ochoa et al.'s 2021 replication, arXiv 2110.07889). These are *published, consumed* APIs under maximal social pressure to stay stable. Pre-implementation boundaries in a solo project will churn more, not less.
- **Hyrum's Law** cuts at the mock layer specifically: consumers depend on all *observable* behavior, not the declared contract. Children coding against a sibling mock will absorb the mock's incidental shapes and values; when the real sibling differs in undeclared ways, the freeze protected nothing.
- **Tyranny of the dominant decomposition** (Tarr, Ossher, Harrison, Sutton, ICSE 1999): any single tree scatters the concerns that don't align with it. The fission triggers (shared oracle, aspect uniformity, load-bearingness) choose one decomposition dimension early; cross-cutting concerns — error handling, auth, observability, performance — will smear across frozen boundaries and be exactly the changes that trigger "restructuring events."

The record does *not* say interface-first is worthless — modern API-first microservice practice works — but it works with versioning, contract testing, and cheap renegotiation, never with a freeze. Where API-first succeeds, the interface is a continuously re-verified agreement, not a fixed input.

## 4. Integration-test ownership: the glue leaf's strongest suit

This is where the design aligns best with the record. The pyramid-vs-trophy debate (Fowler/Cohn pyramid; Kent C. Dodds's Testing Trophy, 2018; Spotify's honeycomb; Rauch's "Write tests. Not too many. Mostly integration.") has converged on integration tests as the highest-confidence-per-cost layer for typical services. More specifically, Software Engineering at Google (ch. 14, "Larger Testing") documents the failure mode the glue leaf prevents — larger tests rot precisely because nobody owns them — and prescribes what the glue leaf implements: "Larger tests must have documented owners," with project-level integration tests owned by an identified engineer. Making composition a first-class task with its own owned suite is a genuine match to practice that worked. Two cautions from the same record: (a) Rainsberger's combinatorics argument — an integrated suite cannot carry coverage; keep the glue suite a thin journey/smoke layer and push breadth into per-boundary contract tests; (b) suites that are red for a long time (against sibling mocks, by design) normalize failure — GOOS itself keeps not-yet-passing acceptance tests in a separate "in progress" suite so the main build stays meaningfully green. The glue leaf's red suite needs the same quarantine plus a burn-down expectation, or red becomes noise.

## 5. Top-down decomposition as planning: brittleness is the documented norm

- **HTN planning** (Erol/Hendler/Nau; Georgievski & Aiello's 2015 AIJ survey) is the formal twin of this scheduler, and its documented weakness is the same input: hand-authored method libraries. Completeness and optimality are lost when the method library is incomplete or wrong, and HTN degrades badly in domains where knowledge is uncertain — which "software design before implementation knowledge" is, per Parnas.
- **Re-decomposition cost.** Nebel & Koehler (IJCAI 1993; AIJ 1995) proved that conservative plan modification is *not* provably cheaper than replanning and can be strictly harder — a direct caution against assuming the tree can be cheaply patched when wrong. The practical planning literature (plan repair vs replanning) finds repair sometimes wins but not reliably.
- **WBS critiques** echo it at the management layer: full upfront decomposition creates an illusion of independent, linear tasks; the 100% rule forces early enumeration of work that will be re-enumerated; rolling-wave / progressive elaboration is the standard mitigation.
- There is no literature showing decomposition quality is predictable before doing the work; the literature that exists (Parnas 1986, HTN, WBS) says the opposite.

## Verdict summary

**Validated by the record:**
1. **Executable end-to-end artifact before feature work** — Cockburn, GOOS, tracer bullets all confirm the value of early wiring, build, deploy, and end-to-end test infrastructure.
2. **Glue as an owned, first-class task with its own suite** — matches SWE-at-Google's documented-owner prescription for larger tests and the trophy/honeycomb consensus that integration is the highest-value layer.
3. **Fission by anticipated change/validation cohesion** — clustering by shared oracle is a defensible modern reading of Parnas's information-hiding criterion (decompose around what is validated/changed together).
4. **Boundary changes as visible events, not silent edits** — the semver record shows silent breaking changes are the disease; making them loud is right.

**Documented failures this walks into:**
1. **Whole-tree mocking before any implementation** = 1970s top-down stubbing: stub proliferation, stub infidelity, defects hidden until replacement. A fully mocked skeleton validates almost nothing architecturally.
2. **PLAN-authored static mocks with no verification obligation** = the exact stale-mock mechanism named by Google, Spadini et al., and the Pact literature; LLM agents demonstrably over-mock already, and MAST (Cemri et al., arXiv 2503.13657) finds specification error (41.8%) and inter-agent misalignment (36.9%) dominate multi-agent failures — the PLAN-writes-mocks step concentrates both.
3. **Interface freeze at interface fidelity** contradicts Parnas 1986 (correct decomposition before implementation knowledge is not possible), the CORBA record (spec-before-implementation yields bloated wrong interfaces), and Maven-scale evidence that ~1/3 of even shipped releases break interfaces. Hyrum's Law means the freeze won't even protect what it claims: children will couple to mock incidentals.
4. **Early full-tree fission** meets the tyranny of the dominant decomposition and HTN method-library brittleness; Nebel & Koehler warn that patching a wrong tree is not cheaper than rebuilding it.

**Specific amendments:**
1. **Contracts, not static mocks.** Each boundary mock must be a projection of a contract suite that is replayed against the real leaf the moment it exists (Pact-style dual execution, or Google-style record/replay once one real path exists). Mock/real divergence becomes a scheduler fault, not a silent pass.
2. **Skeleton one real path first.** Before breadth-mocking the tree, drive the single most load-bearing path to *real* end-to-end execution (Cockburn/GOOS/tracer form). Let that path burn in the deploy/build/test loop and falsify the top-level architecture; then breadth-mock the rest.
3. **Downgrade "freeze" to "renegotiation with a price list."** Keep sibling-visible changes loud, but make the restructuring event a normal, cheap, first-class move with an audit trail (Parnas's "fake the rational process" — record the rationale, allow the backtrack). Budget for ~1/3 of boundaries changing post-contact rather than treating each as an anomaly.
4. **Keep mocks behaviorally minimal** (shaped types, boring values, no incidental invariants) to reduce Hyrum-coupling; lint children for assertions on mock incidentals.
5. **Glue suites: thin and quarantined.** Journey-level tests only in the glue leaf; combinatorial coverage lives in per-boundary contract tests (Rainsberger's split). Red-by-design integration tests sit in an in-progress suite excluded from the green gate, with staleness/age tracked.
6. **Defer fission below the frontier.** Decompose a node into children only when it is next for deepening (rolling-wave), not tree-wide at interface time — the record gives no support for whole-tree decomposition quality, and repair is not provably cheaper than re-planning.
