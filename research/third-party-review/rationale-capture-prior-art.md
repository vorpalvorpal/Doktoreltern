# Prior-art review: structured rationale capture for an LLM-driven R&D memory substrate

> Research subagent report, 2026-07-11, commissioned for the mid-flight third-party review.
> External research only (no local files read). Sister reports: git-backed-store-prior-art.md, agent-orchestration-prior-art.md.

## 1. Why heavyweight rationale capture died, and why ADRs didn't

**What the record shows.** The failure of IBIS-family systems is one of the best-documented negative results in CSCW. Conklin & Begeman's gIBIS (TOIS, 1988) generated early enthusiasm, but by Conklin's own retrospective ("Facilitated Hypertext for Collective Sensemaking: 15 Years on from gIBIS," Hypertext '01, with Selvin, Buckingham Shum, Sierhuis) the verdict was blunt: "the failure of so many hypertext DR systems to be adopted" was "symptomatic" of a general problem. The named mechanism: "the representational demands of parsing ideas into discrete nodes, with distinctive names and types, seemed to impede the flow of thought, and the resultant structures were hard to change." Their summary lesson: structuring ideas hypertextually is "a whole new literacy," and systems demanding it must deliver benefits fast or users quit. Notably, one gIBIS field study documented resistance *in the parts of the organization not using gIBIS* to concept maps as a communication medium — the artifact itself was illegible to outsiders.

Shipman & Marshall ("Formality Considered Harmful," CSCW journal 1999) generalized this across five system classes into four failure mechanisms: **cognitive overhead**, **tacit knowledge** (people can't articulate what they know), **premature structure** (forced commitment before understanding exists), and **situational structure** (structure built for one situation doesn't serve later ones). Buckingham Shum & Hammond ("Argumentation-based design rationale: what use at what cost?", IJHCS 1994) reviewed the empirical base and found the *usefulness* claim only partly supported — notation both augments and impedes reasoning — and the *usability* claim unsupported. Karsenty (CHI 1996) tested the consumption side: six professional designers reading QOC documents of a past design found only about half their design-rationale questions answered — roughly a quarter of all their questions. Horner & Atwood (NordiCHI 2006) systematized the barriers as cognitive, capture, retrieval, and usage limitations rooted in the "dynamic and contextual nature of design," plus organizational limits (Grudin's 1994 CACM work-benefit disparity: the person doing the capture labor is not the person who benefits, and the benefit is speculative and deferred).

Compendium survived only by a strategic retreat: IBIS shrank to **three** elements (Questions, Ideas, Arguments), and — critically — "only the Compendium practitioner need actively engage with the formalisms during a session." A trained human facilitator became the formalism's sole operator, in real time, with immediate value (the meeting itself goes better). ADRs (Nygard 2011) survived for analogous reasons: a three-to-four-section prose template, markdown in the repo, written by the decider at decision time, *superseded rather than edited*, no tooling, no ontology. Even so, adoption is fragile: Buchgeher et al.'s MSR study of GitHub (IEEE Access, 2023; 921 repos) found ~50% of ADR-adopting repos contain fewer than five records — an initial burst, then abandonment.

**Implications.**
- *Partial confirmation:* the project's design matches the two features that let capture survive at all — a single "facilitator" (the LLM) operates the formalism, and records are append-only/superseded like ADRs, not edited like wikis.
- *Risk (Karsenty):* the historical failure was never only capture cost. Even competently captured QOC answered ~25% of later questions, because rationale needed later is not the rationale that felt salient at decision time. LLM authorship does nothing for this mismatch; it may worsen it by recording what is *easy to generate* rather than what will be *asked*.
- *Risk (situational structure):* Shipman & Marshall's "premature structure" applies directly to numbered inline markers frozen into append-only comments — structure committed at write time, unchangeable, consumed in situations the writer couldn't anticipate.
- *Risk (gIBIS field study):* the artifact's legibility to the one human matters more than to the agents. gIBIS maps repelled everyone outside the priesthood; a marker-dense comment thread has the same property for future-you.

## 2. Does "the LLM does the capture" dissolve the objection?

**What the record shows.** It dissolves *half* of it — the authoring-cost half — and the evidence for even that is early-stage. Dhar, Vaidhyanathan & Varma ("Can LLMs Generate Architectural Design Decisions?", ICSA 2024, arXiv:2403.01709) found GPT-4 zero-shot generates "relevant and accurate" decisions but "falls short of human-level performance"; the follow-on DRAFT work (arXiv:2504.08207, 2025) and "Context Matters" (arXiv:2604.03826, 2026; 750-repo corpus) refine this — the latter finds a recency window of just **3–5 prior records** is the best context strategy, with retrieval-augmented approaches giving *no statistically significant advantage*, and frames LLMs as "mitigating authoring overhead," not eliminating the cost problem. Practitioner reports (Equal Experts, 2024–25) are sharper on failure modes: AI-drafted ADRs **hallucinated reference material — nonexistent APIs, pages, features** — and "if ADRs are wrong, they quickly become noise."

Two deeper problems are documented and unsolved. First, **faithfulness**: Turpin et al. ("Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think," NeurIPS 2023) showed model-stated reasoning systematically misrepresents the actual causes of model outputs. An LLM-written rationale is a plausible narrative, not a causal record; an archive of them is an archive of confabulation-shaped text that *looks* like institutional memory. Second, **consumption at volume**: the Grudin asymmetry had two sides, and cheap generation inflates exactly the side that was already weak. The 2025–26 "context rot" literature (Chroma's July 2025 report; subsequent agent-memory surveys, e.g. arXiv:2603.07670) documents accuracy degradation with context length *even when the relevant information is present*, with enterprise post-mortems attributing most long-horizon agent failures to context drift rather than context exhaustion. Nothing yet measures the downstream utility of LLM-written rationale corpora months later — Lore (arXiv:2603.15566, March 2026), the closest cousin to this project (structured decision records in git trailers *for* AI agents), explicitly offers only "an empirical validation path," no results.

**Implications.**
- *Confirmation, qualified:* capture cost genuinely drops; that objection is real and really weakened. But it was never the whole objection.
- *Risk:* "Context Matters" found the best consumers of an ADR log read only the **last 3–5 records**. That is quiet empirical support for aggressive folding — and quiet evidence against the value of a deep, richly-marked history: even purpose-built retrieval over it added nothing significant.
- *Risk:* plausible-but-wrong rationale is worse than absent rationale, because it terminates future inquiry with false confidence. The project needs a mechanism that distinguishes "reason reconstructed by the model" from "reason verified against an artifact/test" — the `v`/`art` markers could carry this, but only if something enforces the distinction.
- *Evidence flag:* everything here is 2024–2026 preprints and generation benchmarks. No study exists of an LLM both writing and later *re-reading* its own structured rationale at scale. This project is the experiment, not an application of settled findings.

## 3. Vocabulary sprawl: do fine-grained taxonomies survive use?

**What the record shows.** No. The pattern is forty years old and monotone. Trigg's TEXTNET defined 80+ link types; in NoteCards, users "tended not to assign types to links, lest they choose the wrong type," and per Bernstein the demonstration was so decisive that designers largely abandoned typed links. Conklin's own trajectory ran gIBIS's node-and-link vocabulary down to Compendium's three IBIS types, operated by one trained person. Semantic-wiki studies (Kousetti et al., WikiSym 2008) found typed-link interfaces fall between stools — too complex for novices, no better than markup for experts — with limited reuse of ontology elements even with convergence tooling. Folksonomy research (Golder & Huberman, J. Info. Science 2006) shows unconstrained vocabularies converge to a power law where a small head of tags dominates. What survives, repeatedly: **3–5 categories** (IBIS's three; ADR's context/decision/consequences; the dominant tag head).

The novel question is whether LLM writers change this. Early evidence says they exhibit their *own* collapse modes: closed-set LLM annotation shows "instruction-induced label collapse" — high apparent consistency produced by fallback-label dominance and systematic underrepresentation of rare categories (MultiSoc-4D, arXiv:2605.06940; "Where LLM Annotators Fail," arXiv:2605.27913, showing per-class reliability varies widely). An agent will *apply* all fifteen marker types without complaint — mechanical compliance is not the failure mode. The failure mode is semantic: boundary categories (`alt` vs `de` vs `fd`; `opt` vs `fd`) drift, the frequent categories absorb the rare ones, and apparent taxonomy discipline masks label noise that only surfaces when someone queries by type.

**Implications.**
- *Risk, high confidence:* the marker vocabulary (7 typed markers + gauges + aspects + per-move facets) sits at 3–5× the size of every taxonomy that has ever survived contact with users. LLM authorship converts "won't apply it" into "applies it inconsistently while looking consistent" — arguably harder to detect.
- *Sharp test to demand:* type-conditioned retrieval is the only justification for typing. If no consumer ever queries "all `de` across nodes" and acts differently than it would on untyped text, the type is dead weight. Instrument this before defending the vocabulary.
- *Mitigation with precedent:* Golder & Huberman-style convergence suggests letting the fold *report* per-type usage frequencies and merging types whose usage distributions overlap. Plan for the taxonomy to shrink; don't defend it at current size.

## 4. Append-only + latest-wins fold

**What the record shows.** The pattern is well-precedented (event sourcing, Datomic's "accumulate, don't update," Kafka compacted topics) and its pains are equally well-catalogued. Overeem et al. ("An empirical characterization of event sourced systems and their schema evolution — lessons from industry," JSS 2021; 25 systems + practitioner interviews) name five: **schema/event evolution** (old records lack fields new readers expect; upcasters vs. versioned types), steep learning curve, tooling gaps, **projection rebuild cost** (nonlinear growth; "replay storms"), and data deletion/privacy. Practitioner literature (Doomen's "The Ugly of Event Sourcing" series) adds read-model drift and the human-facing "what's current?" confusion — the raw log and the folded view disagree in exactly the moments that matter. Datomic works because the platform itself provides the canonical current view and as-of queries; a hand-rolled MCP fold is reimplementing that guarantee without its testing surface.

The LLM-specific evidence is newer and more alarming: "Governance Decay" (arXiv:2606.22528, 2026) shows that when compaction/summarization drops a standing in-context constraint, agent policy violations jump from 0% to 30–38% (up to 59% for some models), and stay at 0% when the constraint survives. Latest-wins folding is structurally the same operation: anything standing-but-not-recently-restated (open `v` requirements, `de` warnings) is precisely what a recency-biased fold silences. Related 2026 work (Slipstream, arXiv:2605.08580; provenance-aware tiered memory, arXiv:2602.17913) exists because unvalidated compaction demonstrably loses load-bearing content.

**Implications.**
- *Confirmation:* append-only with supersession matches both ADR practice (supersede, don't edit) and event-store practice; it is the right substrate choice, and better-precedented than the marker vocabulary sitting on top of it.
- *Risk, the big one:* "latest wins" is only safe for *state*; it is unsafe for *obligations*. Governance Decay is a direct quantitative warning: standing constraints must be **pinned** through the fold (constraint pinning is the paper's mitigation), not left to recency. Open `v` items and dead-end warnings need explicit survival rules, not latest-wins.
- *Risk:* schema evolution will hit the marker grammar itself. Every grammar change strands old comments in old syntax; Overeem et al. found this the top pain even with disciplined teams. Version the grammar in-band from day one and budget for upcasting.
- *Risk:* fold correctness has no oracle. Event-sourced shops rebuild projections from scratch to detect drift; the equivalent here — periodically re-folding from the full log and diffing against the incremental fold — should be a standing lint, or "what's current" confusion will arrive silently.

**Overall evidence flags.** Karsenty is n=6; Conklin's retrospective is the Compendium authors' own account of their pivot; the LLM-ADR literature is generation-benchmark preprints with no longitudinal consumption data; Governance Decay is a single-author preprint; label-collapse benchmarks come from social-media annotation, not marker grammars. The strongest, most replicated findings are the two the design most directly contradicts: taxonomies above ~5 types collapse, and recency-based folding drops standing constraints.
